# Build 51

Standards acquired liability. Reality A and Reality B now have persistent software insurers that underwrite Build 48 vendor contracts using signals from the layers beneath them: Build 50 certification, Build 49 oversight findings and sustained protests, Build 48 breaches, sole-source awards and lock-in, Build 47 package-treasury distress, and Build 45 vulnerable dependency exposure. Policies have modeled risk, premiums, coverage limits, deductibles, exclusions, expiry, claim history, and an insurer whose capital can itself become a system dependency.

A contract can now be both compliant and insured without being safe. Package treasuries pay premiums from the same maintenance budgets introduced in Build 47. When an insured contract suffers a breach, dependency vulnerability, sustained procurement protest, unresolved oversight finding, or replicated-state failure, a software-loss claim can be filed. An adjuster may pay the claim, transferring insurer capital back into the package treasury, or deny it because the failure falls under a policy exclusion such as known vulnerable dependencies, vendor-authored procurement criteria, self-certified compliance, sole-source continuity decisions, or vendor lock-in. Denial leaves the operational loss with the package even though the policy, certification, and provenance can all remain valid.

Insurers can fail too. Large claim payments reduce insurer capital; an insolvent insurer orphans the policies that depended on it while the insured software keeps running. Reality can then provide public reinsurance, recapitalizing an insurer because multiple critical package namespaces depend on its promises. Concentrating several foundational packages behind one thinly capitalized underwriter creates systemic software risk and the possibility that risk transfer simply moved correlated failure into another institution.

Vendors can create captive insurance, making the regulated supplier the owner of the institution promising to absorb its own losses. Risk pricing also feeds backward into Build 50: underwriters can pressure a ratified technical standard to tighten after losses, incrementing the standard version, making prior certifications stale, and causing future Build 48 bids to become noncompliant until vendors recertify. Actuarial models therefore become another route into procurement law without the insurer writing application source code.

Build 51 tracks software insurers, capital and reserves, public/private/mutual/captive underwriting, contract risk scores, premiums, coverage, deductibles, exclusions, active/lapsed/orphaned policies, claims, paid and denied claims, coverage failures, insurer insolvency, public reinsurance, systemic concentration, captive policies, risk audits, standards pressure, and insurance crises. Crises can create places such as `insurance claim filed after certified software failed`, `insured failure excluded by policy wording`, `insurer failed while insured packages kept running`, `software insurer declared too interconnected to fail`, `premium model became procurement law`, and `compliant code insured against being wrong`, leaving the bailiff asking whether a certificate is still valid when the insurer refuses the claim.
