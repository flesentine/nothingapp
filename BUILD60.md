# Build 60

The stabilization board acquired treaty law. Build 59 made Build 58's quota numbers operational through weighted directors, borrower recusals, surveillance reports, 60% program-action thresholds, creditor majorities, board deadlock, and a staff-chair systemic-necessity override. Build 60 writes those powers into persistent Articles of Interreality Stabilization. The treaty is not a replacement for the board or the fund; it is a new legal layer above them. Quota representation, borrower recusal, major-action voting, systemic necessity, surveillance, conditionality, and withdrawal now exist as individually versioned treaty articles that can be interpreted, entrenched, amended, challenged, and survived.

The Articles establish seven initial rules. Article I recognizes quota-weighted representation. Article II entrenches borrower recusal. Article III recognizes the 60% major-action threshold. Article IV permits one systemic-necessity act after genuine deadlock. Article V authorizes stabilization surveillance. Article VI authorizes conditionality reaching into package, trade, FX, and monetary policy. Article VII recognizes withdrawal while explicitly preserving accrued obligations. The simulation can therefore distinguish between the operational rule in Build 59 and the treaty text that claims to authorize it.

Build 60 creates a three-seat Stabilization Treaty Tribunal: a Reality A judge, a Reality B judge, and an Archive neutral judge. A user can challenge the latest fund-board decision. The tribunal maps the challenged motion to its governing article and decides whether the act was treaty-valid or ultra vires. Serial chair overrides can exceed the original Article IV emergency limit; conditionality can be invalidated when no current surveillance basis exists; and formally sufficient quota decisions can be upheld even when the borrower had no vote. Crucially, an ultra-vires judgment does not roll back the already-applied Build 59 effect. A reserve tranche already transferred remains transferred, and a condition already tightened remains part of operational history. Build 60 thus creates `legal invalidity arriving after operational finality` and `unlawful reserve decision that cannot be unspent`.

Repeated emergency behavior can itself change the law. If the staff chair has repeatedly used systemic-necessity overrides, Build 60 can codify that practice as emergency custom. Article IV advances to a new version and begins recognizing repeated, openly recorded overrides as customary authority. Treaty legitimacy falls because an exception became constitutional text through repetition rather than through clean ex ante agreement. Before codification, a serial override can be declared ultra vires; after codification, the same kind of later override can be upheld. The harness verifies exactly this transition: a first serial override was invalidated, Article IV became version 2 customary law, and a later override was then treated as treaty-valid.

Article VII creates formal withdrawal. A member can file notice and then complete withdrawal from the stabilization treaty. The withdrawing reality loses its board director, voting share, RDR balance, and access to active stabilization programs. Pending program motions are barred, and the remaining member becomes the only active voting member. The withdrawing reality receives back as much of its previously contributed foreign-reserve quota as the fund still holds. But withdrawal does not erase Build 57 external debt, foreign invoices, trade contracts, deployed package artifacts, or previous stabilization history. The harness demonstrates Reality A recovering six RB of contributed reserves and leaving the treaty while all twelve units of external debt remained outstanding. The simulation can therefore contain `left the institution but not the debt` and `formal sovereignty restored while foreign invoices remained`.

Treaty amendment and entrenchment make the legal layer persistent rather than rhetorical. Article III can be formally entrenched under the treaty amendment threshold. Article IV can change through repeated emergency custom. Treaty cases, amendments, judgments, and withdrawals are separately recorded, while the Build 59 motion that triggered a case keeps its own operational status. This preserves the project's core separation between what happened, what was authorized, what later law says was valid, and what can still be undone.

Build 60 emits treaty creation, judgments, emergency custom, amendments, and withdrawal into the existing Build 40 trust-audit history. Treaty crises open ordinary observability incidents. Build 60 tracks the Articles of Interreality Stabilization, seven versioned articles, treaty members, tribunal judges, treaty cases, ultra-vires findings, treaty-valid findings, emergency customary law, formal amendments, withdrawal notices, completed withdrawals, reserve repatriation, post-withdrawal board status, program-access suspension, trust-log integration, incidents, and treaty crises. Crises can create places such as `treaty rule written after the emergency it now authorizes`, `unlawful reserve decision that cannot be unspent`, `exception repeated until it became constitutional text`, `left the institution but not the debt`, and `sovereignty clause attached to a foreign-reserve balance sheet`, leaving the bailiff asking whether the treaty constrains power or merely records who already has it.
